I’m a Senior Data Scientist working at the intersection of **data, AI, and real operational problems**.

My background is unusual for a data scientist. I studied industrial engineering and spent fourteen years in operations research and decision-support in the defense sector before moving into machine learning. That experience shaped how I work today: I’m less interested in building models in isolation and more interested in what happens around the model — how the problem is framed, how the data is understood, how the system gets deployed, and whether people can actually use its output to make better decisions.

## From decision support to applied AI

From 2005 to 2019, I worked on decision-support systems involving cost-effectiveness analysis, inventory planning, workforce planning, scheduling, and simulation. I also led the software test division for the ADVENT combat management system and worked on discrete-event and agent-based simulations of naval operations.

In 2019 I moved into data science, working across very different domains: gaming, healthcare NLP, beauty analytics, and automotive retail. At John Snow Labs, I worked on clinical NLP and knowledge-graph-based question answering. At eyva, I built NLP and knowledge-graph solutions for product and customer analytics. At Berlin Tires, I worked across forecasting, analytics, and data infrastructure, taking ownership of the data and AI function end to end.

Those experiences reinforced something I had learned much earlier in operations research:

**The hard part is rarely the model itself. The hard part is turning an ambiguous operational problem into something that can be measured, built, deployed, and trusted.**

## Building for real customer problems

Today at **invent.ai**, I work on data science and AI for enterprise retail, particularly around forecasting, replenishment, and customer data.

A significant part of my work is close to the customer and the delivery process. I work with imperfect real-world datasets, help teams understand what the data actually contains, investigate business assumptions, and turn those findings into analytical and production systems.

This has led me toward a more **forward-deployed way of engineering**: solve the immediate customer problem, but also look for patterns that can be standardized, automated, and reused across projects.

One example is **Pulse**, an internal engineering platform that I built independently to support the delivery of retail customer projects.

Pulse addresses one of the most time-consuming parts of implementation: the **pre-ETL and ETL process**, where project teams traditionally spend significant time manually validating data, checking assumptions, investigating anomalies, and deciding whether the data is ready for the next implementation step.

Its validation and analysis workflows reduce the need for this **human-in-the-loop work by roughly 90–95%**, allowing teams to move from raw customer data to a much clearer implementation starting point with far less manual investigation.

Pulse also runs advanced retail analyses automatically, including **like-for-like analysis, product-launch cohorts, WROS and other domain-specific diagnostics**. These analyses can run overnight, so the project team can start the next day with a much richer understanding of the retailer's data and business behavior.

The other part of Pulse is **pulse-orbit**. Rather than being limited to the initial onboarding phase, it supports the project team throughout the implementation lifecycle.

As new capabilities are implemented, Pulse can immediately expose the corresponding analytical views and diagnostics. When forecasting is implemented, for example, forecasting-related analyses and views become available across the relevant screens. When replenishment is implemented, replenishment analyses and diagnostics are added as well.

This creates a fast feedback loop between **implementation and analytical validation**: instead of waiting until an implementation is fully complete to understand whether it is working as expected, the project team can continuously use Pulse to explore the results, identify issues, and build confidence in each new capability.

That is what I find most interesting about Pulse. It is not simply a collection of dashboards or validation checks. It is a way of making the delivery process itself more **automated, observable, and evidence-driven**.

The underlying principle is simple:

**Automate what can be standardized. Keep humans focused on what requires judgment.**

## How I work

I tend to work across boundaries rather than inside a single technical layer.

A typical problem might involve understanding a business process, exploring an unfamiliar dataset, defining the right metrics, writing the pipeline, building a model or analytical workflow, deploying it, and then sitting with the people who need to use the result.

I enjoy that entire loop.

I also care about the engineering system around the work: validation frameworks, reusable components, documentation, observability, deployment patterns, and ways of turning lessons from one customer project into capabilities that can be reused elsewhere.

That is increasingly how I think about data science: **not just building models, but improving the system by which useful intelligence gets from data to decisions.**
