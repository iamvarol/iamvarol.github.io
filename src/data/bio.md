I did not arrive at data science through a computer science degree. I came
through industrial engineering, and then through fourteen years of operations
research in the defense sector — a longer detour than most people in this field
have taken, and the part of my background I am least willing to call a detour.

## Fourteen years of decisions that had to hold

From 2005 to 2019 I built decision-support systems for the Turkish defense
sector: cost-effectiveness analysis, stock planning, workforce planning,
scenario comparison. One of those platforms served more than fifty thousand
people. I redesigned inventory management and took seventeen percent out of the
cost while holding availability, and applied scheduling optimization that cut
overtime by forty percent. Between 2016 and 2019 I led the software test
division for the ADVENT combat management system, deployed across more than
thirty vessels, and sat on its project management board.

I also built discrete-event and agent-based simulations of naval logistics and
counter-piracy operations, which became two peer-reviewed publications.

What that period taught me is the thing I still care most about: a model nobody
acts on is a model that failed. Every one of those systems had a person at the
end of it who had to make a decision and answer for it. The mathematics was
rarely the hard part. Getting the framing right, and earning enough trust that
the output actually changed what someone did, was the hard part.

## Moving into production machine learning

I moved into data science properly in 2019 and spent the next few years
deliberately covering ground: consulting and teaching at Datajarlabs, where I
also ran a hiring datathon that produced eight hires; player modelling for a
mobile game with millions of users; and clinical NLP at John Snow Labs for
healthcare organisations including Optum, CVS Health and Philips, where I
fine-tuned BioBERT-family models for medical entity recognition and
de-identification and shipped a medical question-answering system over a
clinical knowledge graph.

Then beauty analytics at eyva, building a voice-of-customer NLP engine and a
Neo4j knowledge graph of products and ingredients. Then Berlin Tires, where I
owned the data and AI function end to end and built demand forecasting that
reached ninety-five percent quarterly accuracy, cut excess stock by twenty-two
percent, and saved around €1.2M a year.

I am now at invent.ai, delivering supply-chain data science for enterprise
retailers. The work I am proudest of there is not a model. It is the
data-validation platform I designed and maintain, which now gates every active
client project — a framework of YAML-defined rule pools and severity tiers that
turns days of manual SQL triage into an automated go / no-go decision before any
dataset reaches a model. It exists because the most expensive failures I have
seen were not bad models. They were good models trained on data nobody had
checked.

## How I work

I own problems end to end: the business framing, the pipeline, the model, the
deployment, and the conversation with the executive who has to trust the output.
That last part is not an afterthought. Much of my time goes to client
stakeholders and steering committees, and I think explaining uncertainty to
someone who is accountable for a number is a technical skill, not a soft one.

I spent several years in a PhD programme at METU researching vehicle routing for
unmanned aerial vehicles, and did not finish it. I would rather say that plainly
than let it sit ambiguously on a CV.
